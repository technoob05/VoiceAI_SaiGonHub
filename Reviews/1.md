Summary
The paper proposes a tonal-perceptual caching mechanism for on-device Vietnamese speech translation. The core idea is a linguistically grounded 272-dimensional fingerprint split into a 6-dimensional tone stream and a 260-dimensional segmental stream, combined with a two-stream voting rule that requires both streams to agree on the same cache entry to declare a hit. The authors report that this design eliminates false hits on a synthetic Vietnamese tonal minimal-pair set, provides large latency gains on cache hits, and integrates with an INT8 cascade for further speedups, along with detailed ablations and several negative results.

Strengths
Technical novelty and innovation

The typology-aware fingerprint design that elevates tone to a first-class signal is thoughtfully motivated and addresses a real gap between music-style audio hashing and linguistic identity.
The two-stream voting architecture is simple yet original in this context, and the argument that concatenation on the unit sphere drowns the tone signal is clearly articulated and empirically supported.
A tone-aware VAD and practical on-device engineering choices (INT8 substrates, cache layering, text-level memory) make the proposal deployment-oriented rather than purely algorithmic.
The paper provides rare and valuable “honest failures,” which sharpen the final design rationale.
Experimental rigor and validation

Clear ablations quantify the contribution of each fingerprint block and demonstrate why the tone component is indispensable at the minimal-pair level.
Multiple measurements with bootstrap CIs and permutation tests demonstrate attention to statistical rigor for timing and discrimination metrics.
Cache scalability to N=1024 entries and a fleet-scale simulation highlight deployment-relevant issues beyond a single-device setting.
Reporting of negative results (e.g., failed single-vector fixes, early-exit illusion) increases confidence in the final architectural choice.
Clarity of presentation

The pipeline is well-explained, with concrete thresholds and reproducibility details (pseudocode, constants), and a clear separation of caches (T1 audio vs. T2 text).
The problem motivation (tonal minimal pairs) is compelling and consistently referenced to guide decisions.
Significance of contributions

The work targets a high-impact, under-explored setting: on-device S2ST for tonal, low-resource languages under industrial constraints.
If robustly validated, a reliable tonal-safe caching primitive could meaningfully reduce latency and cost in domains with high utterance repetition.
Weaknesses
Technical limitations or concerns

The two-stream rule uses equality of argmax entries across streams rather than checking for existence of any entry passing both thresholds; this can depress recall unnecessarily and may be suboptimal at scale.
The tone histogram depends on heuristic F0 extraction and hand-tuned binning; robustness under real-world noise, coarticulation, and creaky voice is not convincingly demonstrated with human speech.
The T2 text cache strips Vietnamese diacritics, risking conflation of semantically distinct forms; even if MT is the only skipped stage, this could still yield incorrect outputs.
The claim of “100% tonal protection” is shown on a small, synthesized (TTS) minimal-pair set, which may not reflect human acoustic variability.
Experimental gaps or methodological issues

End-to-end translation quality (BLEU, COMET, human eval) is not reported; the focus remains on WER and hit/miss rates, leaving the core task-level impact unclear.
The ASR backbone in INT8 shows very high absolute WER even after LM rescoring, undermining practical quality claims for an on-device system.
On-device measurements are not taken on a representative mobile/NPU platform; CPU-only timings may not transfer, and the 25 ms cache-hit latency with TTS seems optimistic without real-device evidence and longer-utterance scaling.
Comparisons against stronger audio-retrieval baselines (e.g., SSL embeddings like wav2vec/Hubert, speaker-robust embeddings, or modern audio CLIP-style models) are missing.
Clarity or presentation issues

Some numerical ambiguities remain (e.g., product-quantization storage “≈200 KB” is unclear whether per entry or total, and appears inconsistent with the earlier 80 KB/entry accounting).
The “unchanged tone-error rate” is referenced but not clearly defined or measured on human data.
The fleet simulation assumptions (Poisson arrivals, Zipf-1 vocab of size 50) should be better motivated or supported with field data.
Missing related work or comparisons

Limited discussion of speech retrieval using SSL representations, spoken term detection, and robust hashing alternatives beyond Shazam-style methods.
Little discussion of approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) indexing or multi-criteria gating (e.g., product of similarities) that could mitigate recall issues without losing precision.
Detailed Comments
Technical soundness evaluation

The tonal sensitivity problem is real and well-formulated. Splitting the fingerprint into tone and segmental streams is a principled response to the dimensionality imbalance under L2 cosine.
However, Algorithm 2’s decision rule is unusually restrictive: it requires the argmax entry to coincide across streams, even if an entry e exists that satisfies both thresholds but is not the top candidate for one stream. A more standard criterion would check existence of any e such that cos_A(q,e) ≥ τ_A and cos_B(q,e) ≥ τ_B. This change could materially improve recall without relaxing precision.
The tone histogram’s reliance on hand-tuned F0 heuristics risks brittleness under real noise and speaker variability. While the tone-aware VAD and noise experiments are suggestive, they do not substitute for evaluation on human minimal pairs recorded under realistic conditions.
Experimental evaluation assessment

The minimal-pair evaluation on TTS is too small (30–36 clips) and too controlled to warrant strong claims like “100% tonal protection,” especially given the simplicity of the tone detector and the lack of human speech trials.
The ASR WER results signal that the overall cascade quality is not yet practical (53.23% WER after rescoring on a small subset). Without translation metrics, it is hard to judge whether cache hits meaningfully improve task-level outcomes beyond speed.
The cache precision-recall analysis adopts a conservative operating point, justified by safety; this is good, but the effect on recall in real streams needs to be validated beyond VIVOS pairings and synthetic mixtures.
The hardware profile is CPU-only; conclusions about device viability and 25 ms hit latency require validation on a representative mobile/NPU target with realistic TTS durations. Quantifying latency as a function of utterance length for TTS would also help.
Ablations are well designed for the fingerprint, but baselines are limited. Adding SSL-based retrieval (e.g., HuBERT/wav2vec2 embeddings with cosine or learned projections) would contextualize gains over learned, robust representations in the literature.
Comparison with related work (using the summaries provided)

The authors accurately situate audio fingerprinting origins (spectral peaks) and LLM KV-caches as orthogonal. The positioning table clarifies complementarity with quantization work.
That said, speech retrieval has progressed with SSL features and metric learning; those baselines deserve empirical comparison here, especially since the proposed approach is hand-crafted and intended for robustness.
The layer-localization result for tone in SSL encoders is relevant; a follow-on could probe whether mid-layer SSL features, isolated and gated, provide a more robust tone stream than heuristic F0 bins.
Discussion of broader impact and significance

If realized robustly, the proposed cache could substantially reduce latency and cost in repetitive industrial environments, expanding feasibility of privacy-preserving on-device S2ST for tonal languages.
However, false hits carry outsized risk in safety-critical domains; the paper rightly emphasizes precision but must also convincingly demonstrate robustness with real human speech and end-to-end quality safeguards.
Storing per-utterance audio for caching raises privacy concerns; while the system is on-device, policy and security mechanisms (retention limits, encryption at rest, opt-out) should be articulated.
Questions for Authors
Why does Algorithm 2 require equality of the per-stream argmax entries? Would switching to an “exists e such that both thresholds are met” rule recover recall without harming precision?
How does the system perform on human-recorded tonal minimal pairs (e.g., multiple speakers, varying SNR, coarticulation), not synthesized TTS? Please provide a human minimal-pair evaluation.
How is “tone-error rate” defined and measured, and on what data? Can you report it alongside WER for human speech?
Can you provide end-to-end translation quality metrics (e.g., BLEU/COMET/human judgments) for cache hits versus misses to validate that speed gains do not mask quality regressions?
Have you compared the two-stream fingerprint against SSL-based embeddings (e.g., wav2vec2/HubERT mid-layer features) for both the tone stream and the segmental stream?
The T2 cache strips diacritics. How often does this cause collisions between semantically distinct Vietnamese forms, and what safeguards prevent wrong translations from being returned in those cases?
The 25 ms cache-hit latency appears to include TTS; what is the utterance duration used and how does latency scale with output length? Please report on-device NPU results.
The PQ storage estimate is unclear: does “approximately 200 KB” refer to the total for N=200 entries or per entry? Please clarify storage accounting before and after compression.
How sensitive are τ_A and τ_B to noise and speakers? Can you provide threshold-sensitivity curves and an automatic calibration procedure for new domains?
Could the tone bins be learned (e.g., via a small classifier trained on labeled tones) rather than heuristically assigned, while staying within the footprint constraints?
Overall Assessment
This paper tackles an important and under-served problem: enabling safe and fast on-device S2ST for tonal languages through a cache that respects tonal minimal pairs. The core idea—decoupling a tone stream from a segmental stream and requiring both to agree—addresses a real failure mode of concatenated fingerprints and is well-motivated. The paper is clear, includes useful ablations and negative results, and shows promising latency benefits on cache hits with an INT8 backbone.

However, the current empirical support is not yet at the level expected for ACL. The strongest tonal-protection result relies on a tiny, synthesized TTS minimal-pair set; there is no evaluation on human minimal pairs, and no end-to-end translation metrics are reported. The ASR WER remains very high, the device-level latency is not measured on a representative SoC, and comparisons to modern SSL-based retrieval baselines are missing. The decision rule in Algorithm 2 may also unduly sacrifice recall. Overall, I view this as a compelling systems idea that is not yet sufficiently validated to support its broad claims. With a stronger empirical package—human minimal-pair tests, end-to-end translation quality, mobile/NPU measurements, and baseline comparisons—this work could be impactful. In its present form, I recommend rejection with encouragement to resubmit after addressing the above concerns.
